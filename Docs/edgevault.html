The Bounded Overlay

Blending full-season strength with recent momentum — and why the circuit breaker matters as much as the blend.

ArcVest Research · April 2026 · 6 min read

Playoff strength isn't full-season average or hot streak — it's a calibrated blend. ArcVest uses 75% full-season Pythagorean strength and 25% "Last 15" games for NBA playoffs, with 85/15 for NHL. This isn't arbitrary. It's tuned to playoff evidence while guarding against recency bias.

Why Full Season Stays Dominant

Full-season strength remains dominant because playoffs reward sustained quality. The NBA Pythagorean exponent (14.3) and NHL's PythagenPuck coefficient (0.458) derive win expectancy from points and goals scored and allowed across 82 games. That captures roster depth, coaching quality, and matchup-adjusted performance better than any short window. But late-season noise — load management, tanking decisions, clinch rest — distorts the full-season average. Enter the Last 15 overlay.

The Last 15 as Turbocharger

The Last 15 acts as a turbocharger, not a replacement. Recent Pythagorean strength is computed from the team's own most recent 15 completed games: sum points for and against over that window, apply the exponent. For NBA, where load management inflates regular-season variance, 25% recent weight acknowledges momentum without overreacting. NHL's cleaner late-season incentives justify just 15% — hockey's physical toll shows in shorter meaningful streaks.

Rplayoff = 0.75 · Rseason + 0.25 · Rlast15   (NBA) Rplayoff = 0.85 · Rseason + 0.15 · Rlast15   (NHL)

The ±8% Circuit Breaker

Unchecked, a 15-game heater could swing strength 20% or more — chasing noise rather than signal. The ±8% shift cap is the circuit breaker. Compute the deviation δ = Rlast15 − Rseason, clamp it to [−0.08, +0.08], then apply the weighted blend to the clamped value. This bounds the turbocharger while letting genuine signal through.

The Shield in Action: A tanking team's meaningless 15-game skid or a rested star's artificial late-season hot streak both get rejected by the cap. The circuit breaker enforces discipline — meaningful momentum gets through, noise gets filtered out.

Blend Behavior Over the Season

The chart below shows how each blend behaves as the season progresses, using a representative team with 0.500 full-season strength (NBA) and 0.520 (NHL). The orange Last 15 line is highly volatile — it's what you'd get if you only trusted recent form. The green Capped Blend is what ArcVest actually uses: anchored to season baseline, turbo-charged by recency, clipped against outliers.

Playoff Strength Blends Over Season Progress

Why These Ratios

The weights aren't guesses. NBA evidence shows recent form predicts approximately 25% of playoff variance after controlling for season strength — load management and injury status create meaningful late-season shifts. NHL data is more stable; full-season goal metrics hold about 85% predictive weight. Backtests confirm that uncapped blends overfit to streaks, while capped versions track playoff outcomes better.

Season progress matters too. Early playoff rounds pull more heavily from the season baseline, where rosters are stable. Deeper rounds amplify the recent-form signal as injuries accumulate and matchup-specific adjustments become more significant. The blend adapts implicitly as the Last 15 window rolls forward through the bracket.

Tunable by Design

The implementation stays entirely in the model layer. Target computes the blend during strength construction, feeding series simulations directly. All parameters live in Config as named constants — no recoding required to adjust the blend:

NBA_PLAYOFF_BLEND_W = 0.75 # 75% season / 25% recent NHL_PLAYOFF_BLEND_W = 0.85 # 85% season / 15% recent RECENT_FORM_N_GAMES = 15 # rolling window size MAX_RECENT_FORM_SHIFT = 0.08 # ±8% circuit breaker cap

This bounded approach embodies ArcVest's philosophy: math over narrative. Full season anchors reality. Recent form turbocharges relevance. The cap enforces discipline. Playoff edges emerge from that balance — not from chasing streaks.

The Path to the Cup

Why bracket simulations reveal what series odds never can — the full probability of surviving an entire playoff campaign.

ArcVest Research · April 2026 · 4 min read

A team can be favored in the series right in front of it and still be a long shot to win the title. That is not a contradiction. It is how path-dependent probability works.

If a team has a 60% chance to win its current series, that sounds strong in isolation. But a championship future is not one bet. It is a chain of conditional events. To lift the trophy, that same team might need to survive three or four consecutive rounds against increasingly strong opponents. Even before we account for matchup quality, injuries, rest asymmetry, or home-ice/home-court dynamics, the math compounds quickly.

The Compounding Problem

Here is the intuition. Suppose a team has a 60% chance in Round 1, then 45% in Round 2, 35% in Round 3, and 20% in the Final. Its title probability is not 60%. It is the product of the whole path.

0.60 × 0.45 × 0.35 × 0.20 = 1.89% championship probability A team can look live in the present and still be priced correctly as a distant long shot in the futures market.

That is why single-series odds are incomplete. They tell you the probability of surviving the next gate. They do not tell you how difficult the road becomes after that gate opens.

What Bracket Simulation Solves

Bracket simulation solves the compounding problem by mapping every plausible route through the field and aggregating the probability of each route. Instead of asking, "Can this team win tonight's war?" the model asks, "What is the full probability distribution of surviving the entire campaign?"

This matters because the market often prices the next series more efficiently than the entire path. A club with a favorable opening matchup may look attractive in series odds, but if the likely semifinal and final opponents are elite, its title path can still be thin. The reverse is also true: a team with a merely decent Round 1 price can carry more title value if the surrounding quadrant is soft.

The Quadrant Effect: Two teams can have identical Round 1 win probabilities and dramatically different championship equity — depending entirely on who they would face if they advanced. Bracket position is a hidden variable that series odds ignore entirely.

Path-Pricing vs. Headline-Pricing

ArcVest's view is that futures should be path-priced, not headline-priced. The right question is never just whether a team is favored now. The right question is whether the market has fully accounted for the sequence of opponents it would need to beat next.

This is the analytical gap that bracket simulation addresses. By running tens of thousands of simulated tournaments and tracking which teams survive each round, the model generates a full championship probability distribution — not a single series price, but a complete map of every path to the title and its likelihood.

The Two-Gate 5% EV Filter

Published 2026-03-15  ·  5 min read  ·  ArcVest Engine v1.0

Every trade on ArcVest passes through two independent filters before it's logged. Most candidates fail. The ones that survive are genuine edges — not just low odds or high-probability favorites.

Gate 1: Expected Value ≥ 5%

Expected value (EV) measures how much you expect to profit per dollar risked, on average, if you made the same bet thousands of times.

EV = (model_win_probability × decimal_odds) − 1 Example: Model says 60% win probability. Book offers 2.00 odds. EV = (0.60 × 2.00) − 1 = 0.20 = 20% EV ✓ Passes Gate 1

The model win probability comes from Pythagorean expectation — a formula that uses points scored and points allowed to estimate true team strength, independently of the win-loss record.

Why 5%? A 5% EV threshold filters out noise while capturing real edge. At high volume, even 5–10% EV trades are profitable over hundreds of positions. Lower thresholds produce too many marginal trades; higher thresholds produce too few.

Gate 2: Positive Edge vs. No-Vig Probability

Even a trade with positive EV might just be exploiting overpriced odds — not a genuine model edge. The second gate compares the model probability directly against the no-vig (fair) implied probability.

No-vig probability = (1/odds_side1) / (1/odds_side1 + 1/odds_side2) Edge = model_probability − no_vig_probability A trade must have Edge > 0 to pass Gate 2.

This ensures the model actually disagrees with the market's fair probability — not just the vig-inflated line. A trade can have positive EV but negative edge if the book's fair odds are stronger than the model. That trade gets filtered out.

Why Both Gates?

EV alone can be gamed by trading huge underdogs — technically positive EV but high variance. Edge alone doesn't account for the actual profit magnitude. Together, they ensure every logged trade is both mathematically profitable in expectation and based on genuine model disagreement with the market.

In practice: On a typical day, several hundred raw candidates enter the pipeline. After the two-gate filter, 40–80 survive. Those are the trades. Every single one cleared both bars independently.

What Comes Next: Kelly Sizing

After filtering, each trade receives a quarter Kelly stake — a conservative position size derived from the EV and odds. All trades are then scaled uniformly so total daily risk stays within 10% of portfolio. No single position dominates the portfolio. That's the final step before publication.

American vs. Decimal Odds

Which format gives you the edge? The answer depends on what you're doing with the number.

ArcVest Research · April 2026 · 5 min read

Odds formats aren't just display preferences — they shape how you think about value. American odds dominate U.S. sportsbooks because they're compact and intuitive for execution. Decimal odds rule in modeling because they make expected value math direct and repeatable. Neither is universally better, but one will serve your workflow more effectively depending on whether you're scanning lines or sizing positions.

This isn't opinion; it's about reducing friction in the two core tasks of sports trading: spotting inefficiency and allocating capital.

American Odds: The Execution Standard

American odds are the default at FanDuel, DraftKings, and every U.S. book for a reason. The +/− sign tells you at a glance whether it's a favorite (−150 means lay $150 to win $100) or a dog (+200 means win $200 on $100 risked). No mental math needed to classify the side.

Strengths: Native to U.S. execution — matches exactly what you see when placing the trade. Compact for dense displays. Psychologically clear: positive feels like upside, negative feels like cost.

Weaknesses: EV requires a conversion step. Implied probability splits by sign — favorites use |−odds| / (|−odds| + 100), underdogs use 100 / (+odds + 100). Payout math bifurcates the same way. For a quick scan on the homepage, American wins. Open a spreadsheet for Kelly sizing and the conversions start adding up.

Decimal Odds: The Modeler's Language

Decimal odds treat every price as a total return multiple. A 2.50 decimal means $1 risked returns $2.50 total — $1.50 profit. Implied probability is always 1 ÷ decimal odds. Expected value simplifies to one formula regardless of whether you're looking at a favorite or a longshot.

EV = (true_prob × decimal_odds) − 1
Implied prob = 1 / decimal_odds
Kelly f = (b × p − q) / b   where b = decimal_odds − 1

Strengths: Universal payout formula, no sign-based branching. No-vig and Kelly sizing flow without reformatting. Consistent across sports, markets, and bet types.

Weaknesses: U.S. readers need a brief learning curve. 1.91 reads as a blank until it clicks as −110. Less visual punch for distinguishing favorites from dogs at a glance.

ArcVest's engine computes entirely in decimal internally — that's why the manifest stores model_odds as 1.91, not −110. It's the natural format for the two-gate filter, fractional Kelly, and CLV calculation.

−150      | 1.67    | 60.0%   | 65.0%    | +8.5%

In decimal, EV is one formula across every row. In American, you convert each line first. The longshot at +500 jumps out in both formats, but for daily portfolio sizing across a 40-pick slate, decimal's consistency matters.

No-Vig Is Also Cleaner in Decimal

Vig removal illustrates this well. A standard −110/−110 market totals 105.3% implied probability — that 5.3% excess is the bookmaker's margin. In decimal, removing the vig is a single step: divide each implied probability by the overround. A 1.91/1.91 market becomes a 1.81/1.81 fair line. Plug directly into EV or edge calculations without reformatting.

Try it: Enter −110/−110 into the and watch the fair probability appear instantly. That number — 50.0% — is what Gate 2 of the ArcVest filter checks your model's estimate against.

The Trader Workflow

The right answer isn't picking a winner — it's matching the format to the task.

Scan in American. Your book shows −135, not 1.74. American matches the execution environment and lets you classify favorites and dogs at a glance before you do any math.

Model in decimal. Once you're computing EV, Kelly fraction, or CLV, decimal removes all the conversion friction. One formula, every market, every sport.

Toggle as needed. ArcVest's homepage cards default to American for the scan. The Scorecard and tools use decimal for the math. That's not inconsistency — it's using the right tool for the right job.

The hybrid approach is what disciplined traders actually use. American for the glance, decimal for the math. Now you know why.
